On October 1, 2026, a new law took effect in Alabama that substantially changed how public universities handle tenure and shared governance. [House Bill 580](https://legiscan.com/AL/bill/HB580/2026) requires periodic post-tenure review, expands governing board authority over faculty employment and academic decisions, and limits faculty senates to advisory roles. In Ohio, [Senate Bill 1](https://www.legislature.ohio.gov/legislation/136/sb1), which took effect in June 2025, similarly requires public universities to adopt new policies governing faculty evaluations, post-tenure review, and program elimination. Although these laws do not apply to private universities, they may also encourage their leaders to reconsider existing tenure protections.

These legislative changes reflect a broader debate about tenure in higher education in the US. Critics argue tenure protects underperforming professors and limits universities' ability to adjust faculty staffing as enrollment and other institutional needs change. There is some merit to these concerns, especially given the enrollment declines and other financial challenges many universities are facing.

These criticisms, however, focus on the potential costs and constraints of tenure without considering the benefits it can bring to institutions. Defenses of tenure tend to emphasize academic freedom. Tenure, supporters argue, protects professors who pursue controversial research or challenge established theories and assumptions in their fields. As a faculty member, I certainly recognize the importance of these protections, but I think that emphasis can obscure tenure's broader organizational benefits. By giving faculty a degree of independence from institutional leadership, tenure can improve university decision-making and help trustees more effectively oversee administrators.

## Why Limit Managerial Authority?

For trustees and other observers accustomed to settings where managers have considerable discretion over personnel decisions, tenure can seem hard to justify. After all, it deliberately limits the university's ability to dismiss certain employees, even when those employees question their supervisors or oppose institutional initiatives. Why would an organization voluntarily impose such constraints on its own leaders?

One reason is that universities depend on faculty to exercise their own professional judgment, not simply carry out decisions made by administrators. Universities exist to educate students and advance knowledge, activities that depend heavily on specialized expertise. Organizational scholars have [long recognized](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.26.3.322) that institutions built around such expertise, including universities, hospitals, and accounting firms, cannot rely on managerial supervision alone to direct the work of their professionals.

Consider a university contemplating a new academic program. Administrators might see strong market demand and an opportunity to generate additional revenue. Faculty might respond with questions about the assumptions underlying those projections, the challenges of developing and delivering the curriculum, or potential gaps between the proposed program and the institution's existing capabilities. Neither perspective, on its own, presents a complete picture. The institution benefits when both receive serious consideration.

Unfortunately, organizational hierarchies can discourage precisely these kinds of disagreements. For example, research on [organizational silence](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-6486.00387) shows that employees often withhold concerns because they fear being viewed negatively or damaging relationships with supervisors. Even well-intentioned leaders may underestimate how their authority influences what subordinates are willing to say. Tenure does not eliminate these pressures, but it can reduce the employment risks faculty face when challenging institutional leaders.

Tenure can also help preserve institutional continuity. Presidents, provosts, and other senior administrators often serve for shorter periods than faculty, many of whom spend decades at the same university. Such continuity is particularly valuable in higher education, where academic programs, institutional capabilities, and reputations are built over many years. Long-serving faculty possess knowledge of previous initiatives and decisions that can help inform current choices. For example, an administrator facing pressure to meet next year's enrollment targets may evaluate an initiative differently than a faculty member who expects to spend another twenty years at the institution. These different perspectives can help universities balance immediate financial pressures against the need to [protect their long-term capabilities and reputation](/blog/are-universities-consuming-their-reputations).

## Faculty Independence Can Help Trustees Do Their Jobs

Another potential benefit of tenure that receives relatively little attention is its contribution to institutional oversight. Trustees are responsible for holding university administrators accountable, yet they rely heavily on those same administrators for information about institutional performance. This makes it difficult for trustees to independently evaluate administrative decisions and their consequences.

Businesses have long recognized the limitations of evaluating managerial performance exclusively from above. Practices such as 360-degree feedback incorporate perspectives from subordinates, colleagues, customers, and others who observe aspects of a leader's performance that supervisors might miss. University trustees face a similar challenge. Faculty see how administrative decisions affect teaching, research, and academic programs, including consequences that may not appear in reports from senior administrators. Tenure can make faculty more willing to speak candidly about administrative decisions, even when their assessments are unfavorable. [Research on university governance](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2008.07.005) suggests that tenure and faculty participation in shared governance can help strengthen oversight of university administrators.

To be clear, I am not arguing that faculty are more objective or trustworthy than administrators. We have our own interests, biases, and blind spots. But hearing independent perspectives from people with deep knowledge of their institutions and of higher education more broadly can give trustees a more complete picture of the universities they oversee. Tenure is not simply a constraint on administrative authority. It can also help trustees exercise their own authority more effectively.

## Tenure and Shared Governance Work Together

Faculty independence is also valuable when professors participate directly in governing their institutions. Since earning tenure, I have taken on substantially more service work, much of it at higher organizational levels and involving greater responsibilities. Many of my colleagues have done the same. This work often requires considerable time and effort with few direct professional rewards. Although increased service partly reflects seniority and institutional expectations, tenure provides a degree of independence when these responsibilities require questioning administrative proposals or disagreeing with influential colleagues.

The role of faculty in university governance has long been recognized. In 1966, the American Association of University Professors, American Council on Education, and Association of Governing Boards jointly formulated a [Statement on Government of Colleges and Universities](https://www.aaup.org/reports-publications/aaup-policies-reports/topical-reports/statement-government-colleges-and). The statement recognizes the distinct responsibilities of trustees, administrators, and faculty, including the central role of faculty expertise in academic decisions and faculty participation in broader institutional planning.

Yet a university can maintain a faculty senate, numerous committees, and formal consultation procedures while concentrating consequential decisions almost entirely in senior leadership. As [I've argued elsewhere](/blog/when-listening-becomes-a-substitute-for-acting), giving people opportunities to express their views is not the same as giving them meaningful influence over decisions. If faculty are consulted only after decisions have effectively been made, or if their recommendations are routinely disregarded without serious consideration, the institution gains relatively little from their independence.

Meaningful shared governance does not require faculty to have veto power over every decision. Administrators and trustees have legitimate responsibilities to establish priorities, allocate scarce resources, and make difficult choices. Faculty expertise should help inform those choices, especially when they concern the institution's core academic activities. A university with tenure but weak shared governance may be accepting the costs of tenure without fully realizing its benefits.

## Independence Comes with Responsibility

The independence that tenure provides can also be misused. Some faculty fail to meet their professional responsibilities, while others resist necessary changes because those changes threaten their own interests. Universities need mechanisms for addressing poor performance, and tenure should not exempt professors from accountability.

The institutional justification for tenure partly depends on faculty accepting the responsibilities that accompany their independence. That means taking teaching, scholarship, and service seriously while also recognizing the financial and strategic constraints facing their institutions. Faculty should be ready to explain their reasoning and accept that their preferences will not always prevail. Administrators and trustees, in turn, have a responsibility to take faculty seriously, even when they disagree with faculty recommendations.

## Easier to Manage Is Not Necessarily More Effective

Weakening tenure may make it easier for university leaders to reduce faculty positions or restructure academic programs. Sometimes those decisions are necessary. But greater freedom to act does not necessarily produce better results. Trustees should be concerned not only with administrators' ability to implement decisions but also with the quality of those decisions and the information on which they are based.

Before weakening tenure, trustees should consider whether their institutions are making good use of the independence it provides. Are faculty meaningfully involved in consequential decisions? Do trustees hear perspectives beyond those offered by senior administrators? If not, the problem may not be that universities have granted faculty too much independence, but that they have done too little to benefit from it.
