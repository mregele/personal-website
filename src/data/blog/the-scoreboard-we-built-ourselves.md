Every semester, I spend a day in a room with a group of faculty volunteers and a large stack of student work. We read papers, presentations, and case analyses and score them against rubrics tied to our learning goals. As my college's director of assessment, I organize the day, and I've come to have mixed feelings about it.

For the most part, it's a fairly quiet day. People read, assign scores, and move on to the next artifact. When someone hesitates, the question usually concerns which number best fits the rubric rather than what the work tells us about how our students are learning. That isn't because faculty don't care. Many of them regard the process as an exercise in box-checking, and in a lot of ways, I think they're right. So they do what the process asks of them and save their real thinking for somewhere else.

The more valuable conversations do happen, but they tend to happen elsewhere. Two colleagues compare notes after class about why students keep making the same mistake. Someone notices a pattern in their sections and quietly revises an assignment. Those conversations may well be the best evaluation of student learning we have. But they're also informal, undocumented, and largely invisible to the institution, as are most of the changes they produce. I've come to think the gap between the formal process and those informal conversations says something important about how we evaluate universities more broadly.

## Why We Want a Scoreboard

Businesses argue about how to measure success too. That's one reason tools like the balanced scorecard exist. But underneath those debates sits a final verdict. Revenue, margins, and customers eventually tell executives, fairly publicly, whether their organizations are working. That's part of what makes business success relatively easy to interpret and part of why boards of trustees find it reassuring. It's also why, as I argued in [*The Universal Hammer*](/blog/the-universal-hammer), business experience doesn't necessarily transfer to universities as cleanly as boards sometimes assume.

Universities face a market verdict of their own in the form of enrollment and revenue. But that verdict tells us whether students came and paid, not whether they learned. No number tells us whether a student received a good education. Trustees, legislators, accreditors, and families still want to know whether we're accomplishing what we say we do, though, and they've understandably come to trust answers that resemble a market's verdict.

So we've built something that resembles a scoreboard. Accreditation and assessment processes ask us to define learning outcomes in advance, evaluate student work against common standards, and report the results in a form that people outside the classroom can review. The demand behind this is reasonable. "Trust us, we're the experts" was never a particularly good answer to the question of whether students are learning, and faculty can be confident about things they've never actually checked. But the resulting system tends to be organized around what appears credible to people who weren't in the room rather than around how learning actually happens.

For business schools, much of that system comes from AACSB, our accreditor. Its assurance of learning standards shape nearly everything we do on Assessment Day. I've participated in two lengthy seminars on AACSB best practices, and nearly all of the discussion concerned rubrics, artifacts, and documentation. Very little of it addressed how faculty might talk with one another about what students had learned. Of course, AACSB's standards are approved by its accredited member schools. In a fairly literal sense, this is a scoreboard we built ourselves.

## What the Scoreboard Crowds Out

The most common criticism of rubric scores is that they provide a thin record of what faculty actually observed in students' work. That's true, but I don't think it's the most serious problem. The bigger concern is that the process can crowd out the very judgment it's supposed to capture. Once evaluating student learning becomes primarily a reporting requirement, faculty reasonably treat it as one. They provide what's asked of them and save their actual judgments for conversations the process never sees. It's an example of something I've written about before: [the system often works because people work around it](/blog/the-system-works-because-people-work-around-it).

The result is that the institution struggles to learn from its own experience. Insights remain with the people who had the conversation. Courses change without anyone recording why or checking whether the change made a difference. When a faculty member retires or leaves, much of what they understood about how students learn leaves with them. We can end up with a considerable amount of assessment data and relatively little shared understanding of student learning.

There's some irony in this. AACSB asks schools to "close the loop" by showing how the assessment process led to improvements in the curriculum. But many of the improvements that matter most may never appear in that process at all.

## What Would Count Instead?

One tempting response is to argue that learning simply can't be measured. But to a trustee, that's likely to sound a lot like "trust us," which is how we ended up with the scoreboard in the first place.

A better response is that universities already know how to make rigorous judgments without reducing them to metrics. Peer review, thesis defenses, and external examiners all work in similar ways. People who understand the field evaluate the work in front of others, they have to explain and defend their conclusions, and those conclusions have consequences.

The hallway conversation has the expertise, but not much else. It's private, nobody has to defend it, and it rarely shapes decisions beyond the people involved. So I'm not suggesting that we abandon formal assessment in favor of informal conversations. The point is to bring more of those conversations into the formal process. That will almost certainly be slower than scoring rubrics, and the results will be harder to compare across institutions. It also asks outsiders to place more trust in faculty judgment, which means the process needs to earn that trust by being visible and open to challenge.

## What Assessment Day Could Be

My colleague Ed, who chairs our economics department, put this more simply than I have. He suggested that perhaps the best assessment we could do would be to set up conversations about what worked, what didn't, and why. We're all professionals trying to figure out how best to serve our students, so it's a little strange that we reduce those efforts to single numbers that tell us relatively little.

That's roughly what bringing the hallway conversation into the open might look like. Assessment Day could become the place where those conversations happen, with the scores serving as a footnote rather than the point of the exercise.

Businesses argue about how to measure success, but the market eventually settles the question. Universities don't have that luxury, and for a long time, we've responded by borrowing the market's form. I'm not convinced it has made us much better at knowing whether we're accomplishing what we're here to do.
