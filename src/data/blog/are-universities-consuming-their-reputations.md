Imagine paying $14,000 for a cybersecurity program bearing the name of one of the world's most prestigious universities, expecting to learn from people associated with that institution. Instead, you find yourself being taught by outside instructors, one of whom had reportedly recently graduated from the very program he was now teaching.

These were among the allegations made by students who enrolled in Caltech-branded professional education programs operated in partnership with Simplilearn, an online education company. In July 2025, following a class-action lawsuit alleging that students had been misled about Caltech's involvement, the university announced that it was ending the partnership. The parties settled without admitting wrongdoing, and Caltech announced additional faculty oversight of its professional education offerings. ([Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2025-07-07/caltech-drops-simplilearn-bootcamp-class-action-lawsuit))

How does an institution with Caltech's extraordinary academic reputation end up in a situation like this? We don't know exactly what went into the original decision to pursue the partnership, but the controversy illustrates an important risk for universities seeking additional revenue. An institution's reputation can make new educational offerings attractive to prospective students, even when those offerings have relatively little connection to the academic capabilities that created that reputation. There's a difference between leveraging an institution's reputation and consuming it, and I worry that universities facing growing financial pressure don't always recognize that distinction.

## The Appeal of New Revenue

Across higher education, universities are searching for new revenue sources. Traditional undergraduate enrollment is uncertain, costs continue to rise, and many institutions are struggling to maintain their existing operations. It's not surprising that university leaders are looking for new opportunities. They need to.

Online education can be particularly seductive. Universities see an opportunity to reach students far beyond their traditional geographic markets, often by partnering with companies that specialize in developing, marketing, and operating online programs. These arrangements can reduce the need to invest in faculty, technology, and other infrastructure, making expansion seem relatively inexpensive.

Consider a university evaluating a collection of new online graduate programs. Market research identifies tens of thousands of students in its state already pursuing online degrees, and financial projections suggest that the university needs to enroll only a few hundred students to make the initiative profitable. Surely attracting a few hundred students out of tens of thousands is achievable, right? Not necessarily. Identifying an attractive market is not the same thing as having a competitive advantage within it. The university still needs a compelling reason to believe those students will choose its programs over the alternatives.

Perhaps the institution has a particularly strong reputation in the field, distinctive faculty expertise, or longstanding relationships with employers. Any of these could help attract students. But if the programs are developed and delivered largely by an outside company offering similar services to other universities, what makes this institution's offerings different?

Even when a university has reason to believe it can attract enough students, that doesn't necessarily make the initiative a sound strategic decision. Recruitment costs, operating expenses, competitive responses, and financial risks all matter. So does the opportunity cost of committing resources to the initiative rather than investing in existing programs or pursuing other opportunities. A program that appears profitable in a financial projection may look considerably less attractive when considered alongside the institution's other priorities.

These problems aren't unique to online education. Universities regularly encounter opportunities to launch new academic programs, establish partnerships, expand athletic programs, and pursue other initiatives promising additional enrollment or revenue. I'm concerned that the appeal of potential revenue can lead institutions to pursue these opportunities without adequately considering whether they are positioned to succeed or how the initiatives fit into their broader strategies.

## Leveraging Reputation or Consuming It?

In a [1989 paper published in *Management Science*](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.35.12.1504), Ingemar Dierickx and Karel Cool explain how some of an organization's most valuable assets cannot simply be purchased. Instead, they accumulate through sustained investment over time and can erode if organizations fail to maintain them. A university's reputation is a good example. It reflects decades, sometimes centuries, of investments in faculty expertise, educational quality, research, and relationships with students, alumni, employers, and their communities. An established reputation can create opportunities to enter new markets, but generating revenue from that reputation doesn't necessarily replenish the resources that made it valuable.

Consider Northeastern University. In the early 1990s, Northeastern was a largely commuter institution facing declining enrollment and serious financial pressures. The university already possessed valuable resources, including its longstanding cooperative education program and relationships with Boston employers, but these hadn't been enough to secure its competitive position. Rather than simply trying to attract more students, Northeastern made a deliberate effort to improve academic quality and become a more distinctive institution. It invested in faculty, facilities, and cooperative education, strengthening the connections between classroom learning and professional experience. Over time, those investments helped transform Northeastern from a struggling regional institution into a nationally recognized university. Its existing advantages provided a foundation, but the turnaround required sustained investment to develop them and strengthen its competitive position. ([Richard Freeland, *Transforming the Urban University*](https://www.pennpress.org/9780812295979/transforming-the-urban-university/))

Now consider a university that identifies a promising market, contracts with an outside provider to develop and deliver programs, and puts its name on the resulting credentials. The programs might provide perfectly good educations and generate substantial revenue. But if the university contributes relatively little beyond its established reputation, what is it building in the process?

In some respects, the university is effectively licensing its reputation to another organization. That isn't necessarily a bad arrangement. A profitable program could generate income that supports financial aid, smaller academic programs, or other activities central to the university's mission. Universities also have good reasons to work with outside organizations that possess capabilities they lack. An initiative doesn't necessarily need to develop new academic capabilities to be worthwhile.

The danger arises when universities become increasingly dependent on revenue generated from their accumulated reputations without adequately reinvesting in the activities that sustain them. Over time, an institution could find itself generating more revenue while weakening the very capabilities that made it attractive to students in the first place. There is also the risk of reputational damage when universities offer educational experiences over which they exercise relatively limited control. Students choose institutions partly because of what their names represent. When the experience doesn't meet those expectations, the consequences can extend beyond the particular program. Caltech's experience illustrates how a controversy involving a relatively small part of an institution's operations can raise questions about the institution itself.

One challenge is that the financial benefits of these initiatives are often much easier to observe than their potential long-term costs. Enrollment and revenue can be measured relatively quickly. Deterioration in academic capabilities, weakened relationships, or gradual erosion of institutional reputation may take years to become apparent. An initiative that looks attractive in the short term may therefore impose costs that don't show up in the financial projections used to justify it.

That makes evaluating these opportunities particularly difficult. University leaders need to consider not only whether an initiative will generate revenue, but what it will contribute to the institution's long-term position and whether the revenue justifies any risks to its accumulated advantages.

## Why Shared Governance Matters

The way universities make these decisions is also important. University administrators understandably pay considerable attention to enrollment, finances, and institutional growth. But much of the expertise needed to evaluate new academic initiatives resides outside senior administration. Faculty understand their disciplines, existing academic programs, educational quality, and the capabilities that contribute to an institution's reputation. They may recognize weaknesses in proposals that financial projections overlook or identify opportunities administrators haven't considered. University leaders, meanwhile, are responsible for evaluating competing opportunities, establishing priorities, and allocating resources to support the institution's broader strategy. Doing that well requires drawing on expertise from across the university.

This is one of the reasons shared governance exists in the first place. Universities depend on expertise distributed throughout the organization, and effective decision-making requires bringing that expertise together. Faculty participation shouldn't simply be treated as a procedural requirement or an opportunity for professors to express their preferences. When it works as intended, shared governance improves institutional decisions by ensuring that decision makers have access to relevant knowledge and perspectives. Faculty won't always be right. They may resist necessary changes or prioritize the interests of their own departments over those of the university. But administrators have blind spots too. Faculty concerns about academic quality, institutional fit, or the use of university resources may reveal genuine weaknesses in a proposal or opportunities that have been overlooked. Those concerns deserve to be evaluated on their merits rather than dismissed as predictable resistance to change.

There's also a significant difference between involving faculty while an opportunity is being evaluated and consulting them after major decisions have been made. In the latter situation, faculty may have opportunities to express concerns, but relatively little ability to influence the initiative's direction. That defeats much of the purpose of shared governance, at least when it comes to improving institutional decisions. Caltech's response to its controversy is interesting in this respect. In addition to ending its partnership with Simplilearn, the university established a faculty committee to advise on the strategy, curriculum, and programming of its professional education offerings. We shouldn't assume this means faculty were excluded from the original decision, but the response suggests recognition of the importance of academic oversight. ([Government Technology](https://www.govtech.com/education/higher-ed/caltech-settles-lawsuit-over-cybersecurity-boot-camp-marketing))

## What Are We Actually Building?

The potential costs of poorly conceived revenue initiatives extend beyond programs that fail to meet their enrollment or financial targets. Even a profitable initiative can be a questionable use of institutional resources if it diverts attention or investment from activities that would contribute more to the university's long-term success. Investments in existing academic programs, faculty capabilities, or employer relationships may generate returns that are difficult to quantify, but they can strengthen the institution in ways that additional revenue alone cannot.

Ultimately, I think university leaders need to spend more time considering what their institutions will gain from these initiatives beyond the revenue they generate. Will they develop new capabilities, strengthen relationships, or create educational experiences that reinforce the university's reputation? Will the revenue support investments in the activities that make the institution distinctive? Or will the university become more dependent on income from programs that have relatively little connection to its existing strengths? Answering those questions requires thinking carefully about the institution's long-term strategy and drawing on the expertise of the people who understand its academic capabilities.

University leaders inherit reputations, capabilities, and relationships that took generations to build. Those resources can create valuable opportunities, but they also require continued investment and attention. Decisions about how to use them should be made with an eye toward what the institution will look like in five, ten, or twenty years, not simply how much revenue a new initiative might generate.

**Universities spend decades building their reputations. They should be careful not to consume those reputations faster than they can replenish them.**
